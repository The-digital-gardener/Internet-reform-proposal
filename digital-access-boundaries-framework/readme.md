Consultation Response
Structural Reform of Children’s Digital Access and the Regulation of Converged Internet Ecosystems 

1. Respondent Information
This submission is provided as an individual response to consultation on children’s online safety and digital platform regulation.
It addresses structural risks arising from children’s exposure to converged, account-based, algorithmically mediated digital ecosystems operating on the public internet, and proposes a system-level redesign of access boundaries between minors and the public internet.

2. Executive Summary
Current regulatory approaches are structurally insufficient because they focus on platform categories such as “social media”, which are no longer stable due to convergence across digital systems.
Modern digital ecosystems increasingly integrate:
    • communication 
    • commerce 
    • entertainment 
    • content delivery 
    • behavioural profiling and recommendation systems 
As a result, harm arises not from isolated platform types but from shared system architecture across the public internet as a unified environment.
A further structural difficulty is that attempts to identify and regulate specific categories of online harm are increasingly unstable within converged digital ecosystems.
Many current policy approaches assume that harms can be isolated to identifiable platform types or services. In practice:
    • harmful exposure pathways exist across multiple categories of systems 
    • platforms continuously evolve or reclassify functionality 
    • new interaction and exploitation models emerge faster than regulatory adaptation cycles 
    • globally distributed network environments limit the effectiveness of jurisdiction-specific containment approaches 
Additionally, it is often difficult to determine whether observed harms arise from specific services themselves or from broader cultural and behavioural conditions produced by persistent exposure to converged internet-enabled environments.
As a result:
platform-specific regulation is likely to remain structurally incomplete, while the underlying sources of harm remain difficult to isolate with precision.

Core conclusion
The relevant regulatory unit is therefore not “social media” or any individual platform category, but the public internet as an integrated socio-technical environment.

Proposed policy response
This submission proposes a structural separation between technological education and participation in the public internet during developmental years.
It is important to clarify that this proposal is explicitly pro-technology, not anti-technology.
The proposal does not seek to reduce children’s access to computing, software, artificial intelligence systems, or technical education. Instead, it seeks to separate technological capability development from exposure to converged public internet ecosystems.
The proposed framework establishes:
    • absolute restriction of public internet access for under-16s 
    • full replacement with closed, school-governed digital environments 
    • offline-first and sandboxed digital education as the primary learning environment 
    • delayed first access to the public internet at eligibility age 
This is not a supervision model. It is a categorical access boundary model.


3. Problem Definition
3.1 Structural nature of digital harm
Children’s exposure to converged digital systems produces systemic harms arising from:
    • erosion of trust-based social environments 
    • algorithmic amplification of emotionally extreme or engaging content 
    • behavioural profiling and adaptive content shaping 
    • attention-optimised economic incentives driving system design 
These effects are systemic properties of the public internet environment rather than individual platform failures.

3.2 Failure of platform-based regulation
Regulation based on platform categories is structurally unstable because digital systems evade classification through:
    • rebranding and functional relabelling 
    • modular separation of features across services 
    • embedding social, commercial, and content systems within hybrid platforms 
    • continuous convergence of interaction modalities 
Key structural issue:
Any definition of “social media” or similar categories based on features is inherently evadable in converged systems.

3.3 Convergence beyond social media
Risk is not limited to communication platforms.
Online commerce systems
Large-scale marketplaces now include:
    • algorithmic recommendation engines 
    • behavioural profiling systems 
    • persuasive interface design optimised for conversion 
These systems actively shape:
    • consumption behaviour 
    • preference formation 
    • attention allocation 
Unified ecosystem structures
Modern platforms increasingly integrate:
    • communication 
    • commerce 
    • content consumption 
    • payment systems 
    • social interaction 
into single public internet account-based environments.
Structural implication:
The distinction between “social media”, “commerce platform”, and “content system” is no longer functionally meaningful.

4. Policy Objectives
The policy aims to:
    • eliminate under-16 exposure to the public internet as a developmental environment 
    • preserve and strengthen digital literacy through closed, structured educational systems 
    • separate technological capability development from exposure to public internet ecosystems during formative stages 
    • replace unsupervised access with bounded, institutionally governed digital environments 
    • regulate digital risk through system-level access conditions rather than platform-based classification 
    • support the development of clearer societal expectations around acceptable online behaviour by structurally distinguishing educational digital environments from public internet participation during developmental stages, establishing bounded conditions that enable later independent and responsible digital engagement 

5. Proposed Policy Framework

5.1 Core principle: separation of systems
This proposal establishes a strict separation between:
A. The public internet
A global, open, account-based, algorithmically mediated ecosystem integrating communication, commerce, and content systems.
B. Educational digital environments
Closed, institutionally governed systems designed exclusively for learning and structured digital capability development.

5.2 Absolute restriction: under-16 access to the public internet
Under-16s should have no access to the public internet in any form, whether supervised or unsupervised.
This includes:
    • open web browsing 
    • social media systems 
    • online marketplaces 
    • algorithmically mediated content feeds 
    • any account-based system that provides access to public internet services or data 
Implementation mechanism:
This restriction is enforced through:
    • prohibition on provision of unrestricted internet-enabled general-purpose devices to under-16s 
    • requirement that any digital devices used by under-16s operate only within approved closed educational environments 
Key principle:
The public internet is not an educational environment for minors.

5.3 Replacement environment: bounded educational digital systems
Schools and educational institutions should provide closed systems including:
    • closed, institutionally governed learning environments 
    • curated and pre-vetted knowledge/resource repositories 
    • controlled communication systems within and between institutions 
    • structured collaboration tools 
    • supervised computational and research environments 
These systems provide full functional digital literacy without exposure to the public internet ecosystem.

5.4 Identity-based systems (educational simulation layer)
Within closed educational environments, identity-based systems may be used to simulate real-world digital structures, including:
    • authenticated user accounts 
    • messaging and collaboration systems 
    • workflow and project environments 
    • controlled datasets or sandboxed external interfaces 
Key constraint:
These systems do not provide access to the public internet.
They function solely as:
pedagogical simulations of account-based digital infrastructure
Their purpose is to ensure structural understanding of modern digital systems prior to eligibility for real-world access.

All user accounts within these systems must be strictly temporary, non-persistent, and institution-bound, with no cross-system identity continuity.
Accounts must be automatically deleted on a defined cycle (no longer than one academic year), and may not be retained or used to build longitudinal behavioural profiles.
These systems must be designed to ensure that no form of long-term tracking, profiling, or pre-access behavioural inference is possible prior to eligibility for access to the public internet.


5.5 Offline-first and pro-technology educational design
This proposal is explicitly pro-technology, not anti-technology.
It supports broad access to:
    • computing systems 
    • software tools 
    • programming environments 
    • artificial intelligence systems (including offline or sandboxed models) 
A core principle is that digital literacy does not require exposure to the public internet during developmental stages.
Instead, education should be delivered through:
    • offline computing environments 
    • institutionally hosted software systems 
    • sandboxed or local network simulations 
    • curriculum-controlled datasets and tools 
    • offline or closed AI systems for learning support 
Educational objective:
Students should develop:
    • computational literacy 
    • programming and systems thinking skills 
    • understanding of digital infrastructure 
    • awareness of algorithmic and behavioural system design 
    • the ability to independently assess harmful or manipulative digital behaviours and determine appropriate responses 
    • the technical and institutional understanding necessary to contribute to the development of future digital systems and safer technological frameworks 
The long-term objective is not merely technological competence, but the development of future adults capable of:
    • critically navigating digital environments 
    • participating in the construction of digital infrastructure 
    • improving the safety and developmental suitability of technological systems for future generations 


5.6 Transitional digital literacy objective
Students should leave compulsory education with:
    • functional understanding of how digital systems operate 
    • experience in structured account-based environments and the benefits and harms that can come from them
    • comprehension of algorithmic recommendation systems 
    • readiness for safe independent use of the public internet at eligibility age 

6. Structural rationale
This framework is justified by systemic convergence:
    • communication, commerce, and content systems are unified 
    • platform boundaries are unstable and easily redefined 
    • algorithmic systems optimise for engagement and behavioural shaping 
    • exposure effects are systemic rather than platform-specific 
Therefore:
The relevant regulatory boundary is access to the public internet itself, not individual platforms or supervision conditions.

7. Implementation approach
    • phased rollout across age groups (starting 14–16 cohort pilots) 
    • national standards for closed educational digital systems 
    • institutional deployment in schools 
    • mandatory teacher training and curriculum alignment 
    • parental engagement frameworks 
    • independent auditing and compliance mechanisms 
Infrastructure should be:
    • modular 
    • auditable 
    • institutionally controlled 
    • resilient to circumvention 
The framework requires structured parental oversight and engagement as part of its governance model. All policy standards and approved educational resource frameworks should be publicly accessible to ensure transparency, auditability, and protection against future misuse or institutional drift. 

8. Risks and mitigations
Risks:
    • circumvention through externally accessible devices or networks 
    • institutional inequality in implementation 
    • technological change outpacing regulation 
    • enforcement inconsistency 

Mitigations:
    • device-level and network-level restrictions 
    • standardised national educational infrastructure 
    • equalised funding mechanisms 
    • continuous system updating and auditing 

9. Anticipated structural effects
    • This framework is expected to produce a set of structural effects arising from the separation of educational digital environments and the public internet. These effects are not guaranteed outcomes, but logically consistent consequences with the proposed system design.
Intended system-level effects include:
    • reduced early-life exposure to converged algorithmic and behavioural systems 
    • increased separation between digital literacy development and commercial internet ecosystems 
    • stronger emphasis on structured computational learning within education 
    • delayed entry into public internet environments with greater foundational technical understanding 
    • reduction in reliance on platform-mediated identity formation during developmental stages


10. Economic and strategic implications (UK context)
Implementation may support:
    • domestic development of secure educational digital infrastructure 
    • growth of offline and sandboxed AI education tools 
    • secure school-based computing environments 
    • reduced dependency on global platform ecosystems 
    • leadership in structured digital education systems 

11. Conclusion
This submission argues that current regulatory frameworks based on platform categories such as “social media” are structurally obsolete due to convergence across digital systems.
Digital ecosystems now function as unified, account-based, algorithmically mediated environments spanning communication, commerce, and content delivery.
Therefore:
    • definitional regulation is insufficient 
    • supervision-based models are structurally unstable 
    • harms arise from system architecture rather than platform type 
The appropriate response is:
a categorical separation between the public internet and under-16 access, combined with offline-first and closed educational digital environments that provide full technological literacy without exposure to the public internet.
